Fair housing words to avoid in listings, and the ones that are fine

Lists of "banned words" get passed around brokerages, and half the words on them are fine. Here is what HUD's own guidance says, phrase by phrase, with a rewrite for every risky one. Paste a listing into the free checker below to have its wording flagged.

Updated 24 October 2026 · US federal law, plus pointers to state and local rules · General information, not legal advice

The rule in one line: describe the property, not the people you imagine buying it.

The Fair Housing Act bars any housing ad that "indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination" based on race, color, religion, sex, handicap (disability), familial status or national origin (42 U.S.C. §3604(c)). It applies to the agent and the seller who place the ad, and it applies even when the property itself is exempt. "Perfect for a young family" is a problem. "Three bedrooms on one floor and a fenced yard" says the same thing legally.

What HUD actually says (the 1995 advertising memo)

The most-cited source is a memo from Roberta Achtenberg, HUD's Assistant Secretary for Fair Housing, dated 9 January 1995: Guidance Regarding Advertisements Under §804(c) of the Fair Housing Act. It was written to stop complaints over ordinary words, so it lists both sides, using HUD's own examples:

Is it still current? In 2026 HUD published two notices withdrawing 21 fair housing guidance documents, effective September 2025 (FHEO notice, General Counsel notice). Two of those matter for listings:

  • The April 2024 guidance on advertising through digital platforms (ad targeting and delivery) is withdrawn.
  • The 2021 guidance applying Bostock to the Fair Housing Act (treating sexual orientation and gender identity as sex discrimination) is withdrawn. Federally, that question is now unsettled.

The 1995 memo is on neither list. The statute itself hasn't changed, and HUD's withdrawals don't touch state and local laws. Many of those protect sexual orientation, gender identity, source of income, age, marital status, military status and more. Write to the strictest law that covers the property.

An older source still shapes the "banned words" lists: HUD's 1989 advertising guidelines (24 CFR Part 109). HUD removed them from the regulations in 1996 but has said it still treats their content as guidance. They warned against "catch words" such as restricted, exclusive, private, integrated, traditional, board approval when used in a discriminatory context.

Fine to use (HUD names them or near-identical phrases)

The gray areas agents actually ask about

"Master bedroom"

HUD's 1995 memo says it violates neither the sex nor the race provisions. Since 2020 many MLSs and brokerages have moved to "primary bedroom" as a style choice. It's an MLS-rules question, not a fair housing one, so use whatever your MLS asks for.

"Safe neighborhood" and "low crime"

Not on HUD's list, but they're subjective, often read as code, and they're the kind of comment that turns into a steering complaint. NAR's Code of Ethics (Standard of Practice 10-1) bars Realtors from volunteering information about the racial, religious or ethnic make-up of a neighborhood. Write checkable features ("street lighting", "gated entry") and point buyers to official crime data if they ask.

"Top-rated schools"

School claims are a common source of both steering complaints and plain errors, because catchments change. Write "In the [name] school district (buyer to verify)", and let buyers look up ratings themselves.

"Walking distance", "walk-up", "great view"

Fine. HUD's guidance names "walk to bus-stop", "fourth-floor walk-up" and "great view" as descriptions of the property and neighborhood, not exclusions of people with disabilities.

"55+", "senior community", "adults only"

Only for housing that legally qualifies as housing for older persons. For 55+ housing that generally means at least 80% of occupied units have a resident aged 55 or over, published policies showing the intent, and age verification. A community that doesn't qualify can't advertise this way.

Church, synagogue or mosque as a location

A landmark used to locate the property ("opposite St Mark's") is generally fine. HUD's own example is that describing a facility ("apartment complex with chapel") is fine. What isn't fine is using religion to describe the community or the preferred buyer ("Christian neighborhood").

"Single-family home"

A property type (one dwelling on one lot), not a statement about families. It's fine.

Real ads that fair housing groups flagged

A National Fair Housing Alliance investigation found more than 7,500 discriminatory ads on websites in 2008. Its published examples show how innocent the problem usually sounds:

  • "Looking for responsible adults to enjoy home"
  • "Couples over 55 preferred"
  • "Ideal for 1-2 adults"
  • "Looking for 1-2 quiet adults"
  • "Prefer quiet, respectful professional"
  • "Mature couple or single with no children"

None of them mentions race or uses an aggressive word. Each describes the person the writer wanted. That's the pattern to check for.

It's not only words

  • Photos and models. The 1995 memo deliberately left out human models. Brokerage policies usually cover them: if your marketing shows people, show a range of people across your materials, or show the property only.
  • Who sees the ad. Choosing an online audience by protected traits, or by obvious proxies such as ZIP codes picked for their demographics, falls under the same ban. HUD withdrew its 2024 digital-advertising guidance, but the statute still applies. In 2022 the Justice Department settled with Meta over how housing ads were delivered.
  • What you say off the page. The same rule covers emails, texts, open-house talk and answers to "what are the neighbors like?" Answer with facts about the property and point buyers to public sources.
  • Your MLS and brokerage. Many MLSs have their own word rules, and they're often stricter than the law. Check them as well.

FAQ

Is "master bedroom" a fair housing violation?

No. HUD's 1995 guidance says it violates neither the sex nor the race provisions. Many MLSs now use "primary bedroom" as a style choice, so follow your MLS.

Can I say "family room" or "family home"?

"Family room" is a room type and HUD names it as fine. The problem is describing people: "perfect for a young family" or "family neighborhood". Describe the feature instead.

Is HUD's advertising guidance still in effect in 2026?

The statute is unchanged. HUD withdrew its 2024 digital-advertising guidance and its 2021 sexual orientation and gender identity guidance, effective September 2025. The 1995 advertising memo isn't on either withdrawal list. State and local laws still apply and often go further.

Can I write "55+" or "adults only"?

Only if the property legally qualifies as housing for older persons. Otherwise "adults only" is a familial-status violation.

What about the UK?

This page covers US law. UK listings have a different main risk: leaving out material information. The listing-description skill runs a UK material-information check (price, council tax band, tenure and lease details, and more) for UK listings.
